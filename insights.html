Turning bills into outcomes: what reform takes after the law is passed

Author: Tom Schneider Published: 7 October 2026 Focus: Policy implementation, business process re-engineering

Written as Nepal works to rebuild after the floods of 26 August 2026.

As it seeks to rebuild from the floods, Nepal doesn't have a shortage of good economic ideas. In fact, it has a backlog of 50 pieces of legislation tied to economic reform pending, waiting to become something a citizen or a business experiences.

The challenge is implementation. The Government must pivot from the what to the how.

Of course, not every reform is primarily an implementation challenge. Some, like the pending Nepal Rastra Bank Act amendment, currently stalled over the Finance Secretary's role on the board, involve institutional questions that must be resolved before any implementation work can start. For others, success depends on processes that work consistently, every day, for millions of businesses and citizens, often enabled by effective digital delivery.

Research into e-government reform found that 85% of projects in developing countries fail, totally or partially, not because of bad technology, but because of the gap between how a system is designed and how government works. One response is business process re-engineering (BPR): the systematic work of bridging the gap between a policy destination and operational reality. It redesigns how government interacts with citizens and businesses, prepares organisations to work differently, and helps ensure technology delivers policy intent rather than simply digitizing existing inefficiencies. It's the sort of work I've spent many years doing, advising UK Government departments on programmes that succeeded, and some that didn't.

Having relocated to Kathmandu with my wife, who is a British diplomat, I'm fascinated by how approaches like this might translate to Nepal's own context.

I'll set out some illustrative benefits of this approach using the recently passed Customs Reforms. Changes like this matter because they sit close to the economy itself. Every importer, exporter and logistics provider touches the customs system, meaning small improvements in service delivery can scale across millions of transactions.

  • Focus effort where it matters most: the Bill highlights electronic declarations and National Single Window exchanges to reduce duplications, but BPR work would map what's happening now and evaluate which approvals create the biggest delays, where information is entered more than once, and which process variations create unnecessary complexity. Assessed for impact, we quickly have greater clarity about where implementation effort should focus.
  • Translate legislation into an operational service: the Bill talks about electronic declarations and a single window, but implementation requires decisions about training, regulatory updates, operational responsibilities and system functionality long before the service can go live.
  • Reduce the risk of failure: changes like this involve hundreds of interdependent decisions, and delays in one area can stall progress elsewhere. Effective implementation requires a clear plan, robust governance and early testing through pilots to resolve issues before they become programme-level risks.

What I don't yet know is which of these factors matter most in practice in Nepal. I've set out some differences that strike me as a newcomer, but please get in touch to tell me what other contextual differences exist, including whether near-constant secretary rotation, which has run close to nine months on average historically, is still as sharp a constraint as it once was.

  • Nepal's federal system: any implementation programme would need to examine competing claims for authority at the outset and develop a collaborative decision-making model. The stakeholder responsible for delivery may not be the one empowered to resolve trade-offs.
  • A lack of existing digitization: the UK is ranked 7th of 193 countries in the UN's E-Government Development Index, whereas Nepal ranks 119th. Nepal is rising, having been 125th in the last survey, but implementation would need to recognise that digital maturity starts from a different baseline.
  • Donor funding: historically, customs modernization has relied heavily on donor support, so any implementation programme would need to account for donor procurement requirements, assurance processes and reporting expectations from the outset.

Nepal's best chance of turning Bills into economic outcomes is to embrace the global policy implementation playbook while adapting it thoroughly for local context. The Bills already describe a Nepal that would feel materially better for citizens and businesses. The challenge now is not defining those outcomes, but translating them into services, processes and institutions capable of delivering them consistently.

Tom Schneider is founder of Setu Advisory and has spent a decade advising UK central government on strategy and institution-building. He is now based in Kathmandu.
